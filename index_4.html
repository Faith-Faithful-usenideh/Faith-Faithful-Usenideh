Healthcare · 2026

Hospital Admissions & Treatment Outcomes

ExcelPower BIDAXPower Query

A hospital wanted to know why patients were coming back and where beds were under pressure. The raw file had 984 patient records with mixed department names, billing stored as text and 273 invalid dates, which was 28% of the data.

I standardised the departments, converted billing and dates, fixed every invalid date, then built a six-page Power BI dashboard with DAX measures for readmission, length of stay and recovery.

What I found

  • About 1 in 4 patients came back. Neurology was the highest at 29.5%.
  • Patients aged 56 and over made up 83% of referrals (328 of 393).
  • The average stay was 7.6 days, a solid base for bed planning.

What I recommended

  • Plan bed capacity on a 7.6-day average stay.
  • Create a referral pathway for patients aged 56 and over.
  • Review discharge practice in Neurology and ICU.
  • Validate dates at the point of entry, and collect 12+ months of data.

Short-term rentals · SQL

Where the Money Is in Seattle's Airbnb Market

SQL ServerCTEJOINCAST / REPLACEGROUP BY

An investor asks a simple question: which neighbourhoods earn the most, and does charging more pay off? I worked through 6,442 listings across 88 neighbourhoods to answer it.

Prices were stored as text with dollar signs and commas, and a join was double-counting rows. I cleaned prices with REPLACE and CAST, and used a CTE so each listing counted once. That moved the average from a wrong $257.53 to the correct $235.59.

What I found

  • Belltown leads with $18.93M in revenue at $235.59 a night.
  • The most expensive area charges 4.8 times the cheapest ($625 vs $129).
  • Listings under $700 a night bring in 81% of revenue ($170.0M).

What I recommended

  • Invest where demand is steady, not where prices are highest.
  • Price by neighbourhood instead of one city-wide rate.
  • Compete on occupancy in the mid-price range.

HR analytics · Excel

Why Good People Leave

ExcelPower PivotDAXCOUNTIFSSUMIFS

An R&D department of 961 employees was losing staff. Management wanted to know who was leaving and why.

I modelled the data in Power Pivot and built an interactive dashboard with four filters, so a manager can slice turnover by department, age, overtime and work-life balance.

What I found

  • 133 people left, a termination rate of 16.1%.
  • Staff who worked overtime left at 27.3%, against 8.6% for those who did not.
  • Bad work-life balance pushed the rate to 31.7%. Staff aged 20 to 30 left at 23.9%.
  • Leavers earned 38% less on average: $4,108 against $6,630 for those who stayed.

What I recommended

  • Cap overtime, the strongest driver of leaving that the company controls.
  • Reach the 41 staff reporting bad work-life balance first.
  • Review early-career pay, since staff aged 20 to 30 leave most.
  • Run stay interviews and pay reviews in Q4, before the January exit peak.
  • Follow up with the 154 current staff who rate job satisfaction "Low".
